👯 What is a diphthong (이중모음)?

A diphthong is what happens when two vowel sounds glide together into one sound. In Hangul, each of these letters is literally drawn by combining two basic vowel shapes — and your mouth glides from one shape toward the other as you say it. There are two families, plus one odd one out:

Y-glide family — ㅐ ㅒ ㅔ ㅖ: a basic vowel + ㅣ. In modern spoken Korean, ㅐ and ㅔ sound almost identical, and so do ㅒ and ㅖ — a fun thing to point out!

W-glide family — ㅘ ㅙ ㅚ ㅝ ㅞ ㅟ: start with rounded lips (like "w") from ㅗ/ㅜ, then glide into the vowel.

The odd one out — ㅢ: a quick glide from "eu" to "i" that doesn't belong to either family.

14 basic consonants plus 5 double (tensed) consonants ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ — pronounced with extra tension and no aspiration.

👇 What is 받침 — the "underneath" sound?

받침 sits underneath the vowel — it's the last sound that closes off the syllable. Not every syllable has one — but when it does, the whole word can change! Watch the same first sound + vowel (바) turn into different words as we swap the sound underneath.

Korean has two full sets of numbers that live side by side — here's when each one is used, and a fun sound-change that happens with five of the native numbers.

🔢 Two number systems

The native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋...) and the Chinese-character-based numbers (한자어: 일, 이, 삼...) aren't interchangeable — Korean picks one or the other depending on what's being counted, almost like a habit that became fixed over time rather than one strict rule.

하나 · 둘 · 셋
Native Korean
일 · 이 · 삼
Chinese-character-based (한자어)

📋 When to use each

Used forNative (하나, 둘, 셋)Chinese-character-based (일, 이, 삼)
Age – 살한 살, 두 살, 세 살(formal/written, with 세: 이십 세)
Counting objects/people – 개, 명, 마리, 권사과 한 개, 친구 두 명—
Time — the hour – 시한 시, 두 시, 세 시—
Time — the minutes – 분—삼십 분, 오 분
Dates (year/month/day) – 년, 월, 일—이천이십사 년, 삼 월, 십오 일
Money – 원—오천 원
Phone numbers, order/ranking, page, floor – 전화번호, 번째, 쪽, 층—공일공, 삼 층

Rule of thumb: if it's an everyday count you'd count on your fingers — people, things, age, the hour — use Native. If it's a fixed numeric value or notation — money, dates, minutes, phone numbers — use the Chinese-character-based numbers. Native Korean numbers also only go up to 99, so anything larger always uses the Chinese-character-based set.

✂️ Why do some native numbers "shrink" before a counter?

This isn't just an age thing — it happens whenever these five specific native numbers sit directly in front of a counter word like 살 (age), 개 (items), or 명 (people). It's the exact same shrink whether you're counting age, apples, or friends:

Full formShrunk formExamples
하나→한한 살, 한 개, 한 명
둘→두두 살, 두 개, 두 명
셋→세세 살, 세 개, 세 명
넷→네네 살, 네 개, 네 명
스물→스무스무 살, 스무 개, 스무 명

It also carries into compound numbers — 스물한 살 (21), 스물두 개 (22), 서른두 명 (32) all use the shrunk form at the end, right before the counter.

Why it happens: these shrunk forms are actually the original "modifier" shape of the number — the form it takes when it describes a noun right after it, rather than standing alone. Korean modifiers often contract for smoother pronunciation this way (a bit like "uri" softening to "ul", or "mueot" to "mwo" in casual speech). So 하나 stays full when it stands alone ("하나만 주세요" — just give me one), but shrinks to 한 the instant a counter noun follows it directly ("한 개만 주세요" — give me one item).

🤔 Why is the spelling different from the sound?

Hangul spells the word's building blocks, but when we speak, sounds slide together so they're easier to say. So what you write and what you say can look different — and that's completely normal.

✌️ What is a double 받침?

Normally a syllable has one consonant underneath. A double 받침 (겹받침) has two:

Think of two people sharing one seat. They can't both talk at the same time — what happens depends on what comes next.

1️⃣ A vowel comes next → the second one jumps over

Remember the empty ㅇ at the start of 어요, 아요? It's like an empty seat. The second consonant jumps into that seat, and the first one stays behind.

⚠️ ㅎ is the shy one: in 많아요 it stays silent, so it sounds like [마나요].

2️⃣ A consonant comes next, or the word ends → only ONE is said

There's no empty seat, so just one consonant speaks. Usually it's the first one (with a few exceptions — see the table below).

💡 After the sound is chosen, the next consonant often gets stronger: ㄷ → ㄸ in 앉다 [안따], 없다 [업따].

👀 Watch out: 읽고

Before ㄱ, 읽- keeps the ㄹ instead: 읽고 → [일꼬] (but 읽다 → [익따]). Just remember these two words together.

🙇 Three Levels of Politeness

격식체 (Formal) — endings like -ㅂ니다/습니다. Used in news, presentations, formal business, or meeting someone very senior.

해요체 (Polite, everyday) — endings like -아요/어요. This is the safe, everyday polite level — use it with strangers, coworkers, and anyone older than you.

반말 (Casual) — the plain form, no polite ending. Only for close friends, younger people, or family around your age — never with someone you just met.

쌍자음 (double consonants) look like two letters stuck together — and you'll see them in everyday words like 아빠, 빵, 꽃. Let's learn to read and hear them.

💡 The big idea: tense, not longer

A double consonant is not said twice. It's the same sound as the plain letter, but tight and strong — squeeze your throat and say it with no puff of air.

Try it: hold a tissue in front of your mouth. For ㅋ it flutters (lots of air). For ㄲ it barely moves.

Chuseok (추석), also called 한가위, is one of Korea's biggest holidays — a harvest festival celebrating family, ancestors, and the full autumn moon.

🌕 What is Chuseok?

Chuseok falls on the 15th day of the 8th month of the lunar calendar — the night of the biggest, brightest full moon of the year. It's often called "Korean Thanksgiving," but it's really its own thing: a harvest festival where families travel back to their hometowns, share food, and give thanks to their ancestors.

한가위 (hangawi) is the older, native Korean name for the same holiday — it literally means roughly "the great middle (of autumn)."

🍚 Traditions & Customs

차례
A memorial ceremony held at home, where families set a table of food to honor their ancestors.
성묘
Visiting and tidying up ancestors' graves.
송편
Half-moon-shaped rice cakes, often made together as a family the night before Chuseok.
강강술래
A traditional circle dance performed outdoors under the full moon.
씨름
Traditional Korean wrestling, still played at family gatherings and shown in TV specials.
한복
Many families dress in traditional clothing for the holiday.

🚗 A Nationwide Journey Home

Chuseok is sometimes called 민족대이동 ("the great migration of the nation") — tens of millions of people travel back to their hometowns over the holiday, turning highways into some of the most crowded roads of the year. It's less about turkey and more about the trip home.

Hangul Day (한글날) celebrates the Korean alphabet you are learning right now — and the king who gave it to the people.

📅 What is Hangul Day?

Every year on October 9 (January 15 in North Korea), Korea celebrates the creation of 한글 (Hangul). The date marks the 1446 publication of 훈민정음 (Hunminjeongeum) — "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People" — by 세종대왕 (King Sejong the Great). The alphabet itself was first announced in late 1443.

Hangul Day stopped being a day off in the early 1990s and became a public holiday again in 2013.

📜 What did early Hangul look like?

An early printed page of Hunminjeongeum (the 언해본, a Korean-language version of the preface). This is how Hangul looked in the 1400s.

If you look closely, it is quite different from the Korean you see today:

  • Vertical writing. Lines run from top to bottom, and columns go from right to left.
  • Mixed with Chinese characters. The big characters at the top (世宗御製訓民正音) are 한자. The small Hangul beside them shows how to pronounce them.
  • Dots beside letters. The small dots on the left of syllables (방점) marked pitch — a feature of 15th-century Korean that has since disappeared.
  • Old letters. Letters such as ㆍ (the "heaven" dot) were still in use. They are no longer used today.
  • No spaces between words — spacing was only introduced much later.

The first line of the preface on this page, in early spelling and in modern Korean:

나랏말ᄊᆞ미 듕귁에 달아
Early Korean · "The speech of our country differs from China's…"
나라의 말이 중국과 달라
Modern Korean · same meaning

👑 Why did King Sejong create Hangul?

Before Hangul, Koreans wrote with Chinese characters (한자). They were very hard to learn, so most ordinary people could not read or write. Sejong wanted every person — not only scholars — to be able to write what they wanted to say. His own preface to the Hunminjeongeum says this in plain words.

💵 Sejong is on the 10,000 won bill

A 10,000 won bill (this one is a specimen, marked 보기 = "sample").

King Sejong's portrait is on the front of the 만 원 (10,000 won) bill — Korea's largest everyday banknote. You can see him in his traditional hat and robe, and the bill says 한국은행 (Bank of Korea) and 만원 at the top.

Useful numbers: 만 means 10,000, a Sino-Korean number. So 만 원 = 10,000 won. You will hear this word whenever people talk about prices!

만 원짜리 지폐에 세종대왕이 있어요.
King Sejong is on the 10,000 won bill.
이 책은 만 원이에요.
This book is 10,000 won.

🌌 Vowels: heaven, earth, and people

The vowels are built from three simple shapes based on East Asian ideas of the universe:

Combine them: put a dot beside the person → ㅏ; put a dot above the earth → ㅗ. (The dot ㆍ is no longer used on its own today.)

🔢 Then and now

Sejong's original alphabet had 28 letters. Four have fallen out of use, leaving the 24 basic letters used today: 14 consonants + 10 vowels. Doubled consonants (ㄲ, ㄸ…) and combined vowels (ㅐ, ㅔ…) are made from those 24 — exactly what you practiced in Chapter 1.

🏆 Recognition

The Hunminjeongeum Haerye (the original explanation of the alphabet) was added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 1997. UNESCO also awards the King Sejong Literacy Prize (since 1989) to people and groups who fight illiteracy.
